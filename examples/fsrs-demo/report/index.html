FSRS vs SM-2: what the evidence says

How the FSRS scheduler models memory, how it is fitted to a learner, and what the published benchmarks do and don't show against SM-2.

Research dossier · Spaced repetition · As of 2026-10-28 The short answer takes 30 seconds ·

About this report

Reading the margin

The short answer

FSRS predicts recall far better than SM-2, but the claim that it saves reviews rests on simulation.

FSRS keeps a three-number memory state per card and fits its parameters to the learner's own history. On the developers' public benchmark of about 10,000 Anki users, FSRS-6 reached a log loss of 0.346 against 0.616 for Anki's SM-2. That gap measures prediction, not workload: the "20–30% fewer reviews" figure is labelled as simulation-based, and all of the evidence comes from the people who build FSRS. It is public and reproducible, but not independent.

Findings

The model is well documented. The prediction advantage is large. The practical benefit is not yet measured.

  1. Three variables per card replace SM-2's single ease factor. Retrievability is the probability of recall now, and stability is the number of days until it falls to 90%. Difficulty is how hard it is to raise stability.
  2. Parameters are fitted to each learner by maximum likelihood. The optimiser uses maximum likelihood estimation and backpropagation through time over the review log. Below a few hundred reviews there isn't enough data, so defaults fitted on about 10,000 users are used.
  3. FSRS-6 out-predicts every classic scheduler in the benchmark. In the current tables it scores a log loss of 0.346, against 0.469 for half-life regression and 0.395 for a constant baseline.
  4. Anki's SM-2, as benchmarked, predicted recall worse than a constant guess. Its log loss was 0.616, against 0.395 for the constant baseline. SM-2 does not predict recall natively, so the benchmark built its probabilities from assumed forgetting curves.
  5. Desired retention is the one setting to tune. Higher retention means shorter intervals and more reviews, and the workload rises steeply toward 100%.

How FSRS models a memory

Each card has a memory state. Time lowers recall odds. Reviews raise stability, by less for hard cards and for memories that are already stable.

Retrievability falls along a power-law forgetting curve. The curve drops sharply before the stability point and flatly after it, and from FSRS-6 its decay is trainable. After a successful review, stability is multiplied by a factor. That factor is smaller for difficult cards and for memories that are already stable. It is larger when the review comes at lower recall odds. That is the spacing effect.

Since FSRS-4.5, R(t, S) = (1 + factor · t/S)−decay, with the factor chosen so that R = 90% when t = S. The decay was 1 in FSRS v4 and 0.5 in FSRS-4.5. FSRS-6 learns it per user. For overdue cards, the stability gained from a late review levels off at an upper limit, where SM-2's interval keeps growing with the delay.

FSRS grew out of MaiMemo's DHP model and the SSP-MMC scheduling research, trained on 220 million review logs. That research used Duolingo's half-life regression as a baseline.

Does it beat SM-2?

On recall prediction, by a wide margin. On review workload, nothing has been measured outside simulation.

The current benchmark README has no SM-2 row. The last revision that listed SM-2 dates from August 2025. It scored about 10,000 users by testing each algorithm on later reviews, after training on earlier ones.

Half-life regression beats trainable Anki-SM-2, at 0.469 against 0.513.

Weighting by reviews

Trainable Anki-SM-2 edges out half-life regression, at 0.407 against 0.415.

Reconciliation

Both tables are right. Weighting by reviews shows best-case performance for users with long histories. Which of these two weaker models ranks higher therefore depends on how much history a learner has. FSRS-6 leads in both tables.

Against SuperMemo's SM-17, the only comparison is the FSRS author's own. It covers 19 users, and its error metric is a reimplementation whose exactness the author cannot guarantee. Every performance number here comes from open-spaced-repetition. The data and scripts are public, but nobody independent has replicated the results in the sources read.

What this means in use

Choose a desired retention, grade honestly, and give the optimiser a few hundred reviews.

Desired retention trades recall against workload, and workload climbs steeply toward 100%. FSRS treats Hard as a successful recall, so pressing Hard after forgetting inflates the intervals it schedules.

Risks & unknowns

The gap between better prediction and fewer reviews is the main open question.

  • Workload savings are simulated. No measurement on real learners was found in the sources read, and the simulation's setup is not documented there.
  • SM-2's probabilities are constructed. The benchmark had to assume a forgetting curve for SM-2, which may handicap it.
  • No independent replication. Developers and evaluators are the same group.

Next steps

Measure what matters to a learner.

  1. Find real-learner outcomes. Settles whether better prediction means fewer reviews.
  2. Locate the "20–30% fewer reviews" simulation. Shows its assumptions.
  3. Translate log-loss gaps into workload terms. Makes the benchmark numbers interpretable.
